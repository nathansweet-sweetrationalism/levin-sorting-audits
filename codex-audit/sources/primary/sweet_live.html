Why Michael Levin’s Bubble Sort “Revelation” Is Misunderstood Classical Algorithm Theory Meets a God-of-the-Gaps Argument

ByNathan Sweet February 6, 2026November 1, 2026
Framework clarification and update: September–October 2026 This article predates my later methodological clarifications and remains unchanged for intellectual provenance. My current Recursive Corrigibility Framework and Boundary- and Scale-Relative Realism clarify claim types, scope, evidential burdens, explanatory bridges, uncertainty, and localized correction. Thermodynamic Monism remains a provisional naturalistic countermodel, while my work on consciousness , Whiteheadian prehension , and Michael Levin distinguishes established findings from additional phenomenal or metaphysical claims. Local explanatory success, shared evidence, and unresolved gaps do not, by themselves, establish or refute those stronger claims.

This article predates several later clarifications and extensions of my methodology and working ontology. I am leaving the original argument intact for intellectual provenance rather than silently rewriting the record. The later work makes my claim typing, scope limits, bridge requirements, uncertainty bookkeeping, and comparative standards more explicit.

My work on Thermodynamic Monism was developed as a naturalistic process-relational research programme and comparative countermodel, particularly in response to Platonic and anti-physicalist arguments claiming that biological organization, morphogenesis, cognition, or other phenomena required additional nonphysical explanatory machinery. Its purpose was to test how much of the relevant explanatory work could be recovered from causal continuity, thermodynamic constraint, historically accumulated organization, multiscale dynamics, and higher-level difference-making without assuming the richer metaphysical posit under dispute.

I did not take that comparative success to establish that thermodynamics explains everything, that thermodynamic variables are privileged at every scale, or that empirical success by itself settles the intrinsic nature of reality. The current formulation makes those limits more explicit. The commitments I continue to retain include one causally continuous process order rather than separate causal substances or realms; thermodynamic constraints on physical realization; dynamically maintained and historically conditioned organization; and the reality of higher-level explanatory variables when they earn predictive, counterfactual, interventionist, compressive, or transferable leverage. One process does not mean one explanation.

The same clarification applies to my earlier use of falsification-first language. I did not use the rule “if it is not falsifiable, it is false.” My methodology already distinguished questions of semantic determinacy, evidential burden, theory-ladenness, auxiliary hypotheses, counterfactual testing, and Lakatosian research-programme appraisal. A sufficiently determinate empirical or causal claim should incur conditions under which it would lose support, but not every meaningful claim belongs before the same tribunal. Formal, semantic, phenomenological, historical, modal, normative, ontological, causal, and empirical claims can require different forms of warrant and criticism.

My current Recursive Corrigibility Framework formalizes that plural structure more explicitly. The shift from falsification-first to corrigibility-first is therefore primarily a clarification and expansion of jurisdiction: identify the claim type, determine what evidence or argument actually bears on it, locate the relevant bridge and dependencies, specify what could revise it, and localize failure to the part of the argument that actually failed rather than allowing one failure to automatically propagate through an entire framework.

My current working ontology is Provisional, Boundary- and Scale-Relative Realism . It makes explicit a discipline already motivating much of the earlier work: boundaries, scales, identities, causal variables, and explanatory vocabularies must earn their relevance at the target under investigation. Local success does not automatically transport into universal, intrinsic, necessary, or exhaustive metaphysical conclusions.

An accessible statement of that position appears in What Is Provisional, Boundary- and Scale-Relative Realism? . Explanatory gaps are treated as records of missing warrant rather than automatic evidence for whatever ontology might fill them. Evidence also does not transport across boundaries, scales, or constructs merely because the same vocabulary can be used in both places.

My earlier consciousness work should be read with the same distinction in mind. My proposed bridge between organized physical dynamics and phenomenality was already presented as a risky empirical wager with explicit conditions for failure, not as a settled conclusion about phenomenal identity. Phenomenal identity remained unresolved. What later work makes more explicit is the dependency structure. Evidence for organizational closure, recursive integration, bounded perspective, temporal continuity, report, discrimination, self-modeling, valuation, or agency can bear strongly on those particular capacities without automatically settling whether any of them is identical with phenomenal experience. Evidence earned by one construct should not silently migrate into another.

I therefore now decompose the traditional Hard Problem more explicitly into distinct questions about phenomenal occurrence, phenomenal character, candidate subject boundaries, physical organization, report and judgment, the meta-problem, modal arguments, bridge principles, and explanatory completion. This preserves unresolved phenomenality without allowing the unresolved remainder itself to perform explanatory or metaphysical work that has not independently been earned. The Hard Problem dissolves as a distinct unitary problem if, once those constituent questions are separated, no additional positive explanandum remains and no necessary explanatory relation is lost by the decomposition.

The most current statement of that analysis is David Chalmers’s “Hard Problem of Consciousness” Has a Hard Problem of the Gaps: How His Own Methodology Dissolves Both . The newer argument asks whether the canonical Hard Problem retains any additional positive explanandum after its constituent questions are separated. If no positive residual can be identified, and no necessary explanatory relation is destroyed by the decomposition, then the monolithic Hard Problem dissolves as a distinct unitary explanandum while the constituent questions remain live wherever the evidence leaves them unresolved.

My later comparison with Matthew David Segall and Whiteheadian panexperientialism applies the same rule in another direction. In Where the Necessity Case for Mathew Segall & Whitehead’s Prehension Fails , I ask which explanatory targets actually require subjective prehension rather than merely remaining compatible with it. Mechanistic, dispositional, process, organizational, constraint-based, and intervention-sensitive accounts recover substantial causal explanatory work without subjective prehension as a premise. That undercuts a necessity claim at those targets without establishing that universal prehension is false. Its wider application still requires independent comparative and transport warrant.

My October work on Michael Levin extends a distinction that was already central to my earlier criticism of biological Platonism: strong empirical results do not automatically transfer their evidential authority to every metaphysical interpretation placed around them. In Religion With A Pipette: Is Michael Levin’s “Ingressing Minds” Pseudoscience? A Scholarly Analysis , I separate the experimental evidence for bioelectricity, morphogenesis, biological competency, synthetic organisms, and multiscale control from the additional claim that such systems access nonphysical minds or Platonic structures.

The newer formulation makes the credit-assignment rule especially explicit: evidence shared by multiple rival explanations supports the shared empirical structure first. An additional metaphysical posit earns additional credit only when the feature it uniquely adds improves prediction, intervention, explanation, compression, transfer, or some other claim-appropriate discriminator relative to serious alternatives.

I have consolidated the larger Levin research programme in Biologist Michael Levin Criticism: Science or Pseudoscience? . The index separates the evidential status of Levin's experimental bioelectricity and morphogenesis work from the distinct questions raised by TAME, basal-cognition language, consciousness attribution, Platonic ingress, and stronger metaphysical or theological interpretations. It also collects later corrections, qualifications, primary sources, peer-reviewed literature, and comparative alternatives.

A fuller account of the development of Thermodynamic Monism, what I continue to retain, what I now state more narrowly, and which specific earlier formulations I would revise appears in What Is Thermodynamic Monism? Auditing My Own Framework—September 2026 Revisions and Clarifications .

Bubble Sort Doesn’t Prove God: The Discovery Institute Recognizes an Opening in Levin’s Platonic Interpretation

Original article: February 6, 2026 | Substantial corrections: October 13, 2026 | Additional analysis and material corrections: October 31, 2026

Revision notice: This article was substantially corrected on October 13, 2026, following an audit of my earlier work.

The October 31, 2026 addition examines the interviews, the published study and its public code, and the checked comparison with Joel Dietz, an entrepreneur, intellectual historian and MIT Connection Science Fellow who presented “Radical Platonism and Radical Empiricism” at Levin’s 2025 Symposium on the Platonic Space and who sent me a draft critiquing my argument.

One central point remains clear: neither code-generated clustering nor my incomplete stability explanation identifies an additional Platonic cause. The main audit questions are more specific: which mathematical claims hold or need revision, whether the code matches the experiments being discussed, and whether each citation supports the claim attached to it. I treat my own incomplete, provisionally stated conclusions from February by the same standard.

The dated notes at the end identify the changes and preserve the link to the original text. See the revision note at the end for the corrections, their rationale, and a link to the original text.

Christopher Hitchens would have eviscerated this bubble sort argument in three sentences, but I’ll need more space because the inferential overreach here is layered like a particularly overripe onion. Michael Levin, whose legitimate work on bioelectricity deserves respect, and his collaborators report unexpected collective behaviors in distributed sorting systems built from familiar policies. Those findings deserve analysis. He connects them to Platonic space; the connection is what needs evidence.

Treating surprise as proof of an independent source is a category error wrapped in breathless mysticism and sold as profundity, and the “beyond the algorithm” argument risks the same inferential move as intelligent design when it credits an additional source without distinguishing it from the implemented system (local rules, inputs, scheduling, hardware, etc.).

The title names a pattern of reasoning. Levin rejects intelligent design and agrees that the sorting behavior follows the implemented rules. My criticism applies when an unanticipated result, the absence of an explicit clustering goal, or the failure of one proposed mechanism is credited as support for an independent Platonic source. Those facts do not identify that source. Mathematical constraint, a useful behavioral model and an independently causally active pattern are different claims.

Additional analysis, October 31, 2026: What the finding can establish

Algotype clustering is real. Its particular curves can be novel. Behavioral descriptions can be useful, and mathematical constraints can explain. None of those concessions identifies an independently causally active Platonic source. That last claim needs an argument connecting the evidence to what the proposed source adds.

This distinction also survives Joel Dietz’s correction of my original explanation. My February article overreached by treating algotype clustering as textbook stability. I withdrew that explanation in the October 13 revision, before Joel’s October 25 invitation to comment on a working draft he sent me. Tie handling can still help explain how segregation persists, without explaining how it forms. Joel’s chance benchmark also has a defensible conditional basis.

What depended on my proposed explanation

I offered a mechanistic alternative for comparison: what did the proposed Platonic contribution explain that the alternatives did not? That question does not require my preferred mechanism to be correct. If stability fails, the clustering still needs explaining; its failure does not identify an independent Platonic cause.

I intended these mechanisms as proposals for comparison. But the archived February article used the heading “The Correct Explanation” and introduced its account with “Here is what’s actually happening.” My headings and wording collapsed proposals into conclusions. That was my error: they presented an explanation as established before I had demonstrated it. I withdrew the complete stability account in October. My intended comparative purpose does not excuse the overstatement, and correcting it does not establish the additional Platonic cause.

A demonstrated rival can defeat a claim that the proposed source is necessary. A possible rival can expose an unanswered comparison, but it does not automatically explain the result better. To claim that advantage, my account needs its own derivation, evidence and tests. The remaining challenge can also be asked without endorsing any particular rival: what establishes the additional causal contribution? Uncertainty among explanations does not award the result to one of them.

This also keeps the testability question precise. Specific claims about sorting behavior can be tested, and Levin offers conditions for abandoning his research programme. My objection concerns whether those tests support the independent causal source. That requires an argument connecting the result to what the proposed source contributes beyond serious rivals. Calling the whole programme unfalsifiable would obscure this distinction.

The task is not the complete algorithm

There are several different objects being called “the algorithm”: the instruction to sort numerical values; the actual local policies, state variables, scheduling and stopping rules; and the trajectory produced by those rules from a particular starting state. The sorting goal does not specify every feature of that trajectory. A complete implementation can determine features that its designer never listed as goals.

In the Lex Fridman clip, Levin accepts that someone could track the clustering through the algorithm. He then describes the additional behavior as “neither prescribed nor forbidden by the algorithm” (14:46–15:28 in the clip; 02:18:32 onward in the full interview). That wording needs a stable referent. Clustering may be unspecified by the sorting task while remaining a consequence of the complete implemented dynamics. Moving between those senses makes a consequence of the rules sound as though it occupies a causal vacancy between them.

Write the implemented update schematically as x(t + 1) = F(x(t), u(t)), where x includes cell values, types and internal state, and u includes the scheduled choices and any random input. Once these are fixed, the model fixes the trajectory. Aggregation is another function of that state, A(t) = g(x(t)). There need be no instruction saying “maximize g” for g to rise. This does not make every consequence obvious, easy to prove, or computationally cheap to predict.

The published study makes a related inference when it says clustering is “not something that can be predicted a priori,” because cells do not read algotype information (Section 4.4). But type already determines the policy a cell executes. Policy affects its movements; movements affect who becomes adjacent. The causal route can run through behavior without a direct query for a neighbor’s type. Absence of such a query does not establish an impossibility of prediction. The authors themselves investigate an efficiency-based explanation. Sorting paper.

This is why the six-line textbook routine cannot serve as a complete specification of the mixed experiment. The paper openly describes the distributed architecture; Levin also explains that change in the Lex interview. The criticism concerns what follows from that architecture, rather than a suggestion that the authors concealed it.

There is a further reason to inspect the implementation before accepting broad claims about the absence of defect handling. In the authors’ public repository, the Insertion cell’s prefix check explicitly tests for CellStatus.FREEZE, resets its comparison value, and skips that cell. An isolated execution of that method allows movement with the left prefix [3, frozen 1]; removing the freeze-specific branch blocks it. This is a concrete response to the defect status. It establishes neither a planner nor an exhaustive explanation of fault tolerance. The inspected code version postdates the paper’s first online publication, so it does not by itself establish which version generated the published data. It does show why “no explicit repair planner” and “no implemented response to a defective cell” must be kept distinct.

Clustering formation and clustering persistence are different questions

My original stability explanation conflated them. A globally stable sort preserves the starting relative order of every equal-key record. Strict comparisons alone do not guarantee that property in a mixed system containing nonadjacent swaps. In [2a, 2b, 1c], swapping the first and last records produces [1c, 2b, 2a]. The equal records have reversed without being directly swapped with each other.

In the replay of Joel’s model, every one of the 90 duplicate-key runs reversed some equal-key records. The mixed model therefore lacks the global stability my complete explanation would require. Clustering with unique values also needs an explanation independent of equal-key preservation. Those objections to my old account stand.

But duplicate keys still loosen the final ordering constraint. Different policies can create segregation during sorting, and strict tie handling can help retain it when numerical sorting is complete. Continuing the actual final cells with their existing internal states and unchanged strict rules produced no further swaps in all 105 replayed runs. This establishes persistence at those completed endpoints in the tested implementation.

At ten copies per key, the mixed model’s mean final aggregation was 0.6592. Applying a globally stable numerical sort to the same starting records instead gave 0.503333. Uniformly permuting algotypes only within the final equal-value blocks, while preserving each block’s type counts, gave an exact conditional mean of 0.485117. Numerical sortedness survives this last operation while much of the measured segregation does not. The arrangement within tied blocks matters.

These comparisons distinguish formation from retention. They do not establish strict tie handling as the unique necessary mechanism, and the within-block permutation is not Joel’s separate experiment allowing equal-key swaps. Nor is replaying his sequential model an independent replication of the original threaded implementation. What survives my first explanation is this narrower role in persistence. The complete stability explanation remains withdrawn. Pinned model and experiment.

What Joel’s “flat 0.50 prediction” gets right, and what it assumes

My question to Joel was an attribution question: “Is this another critic’s claim, or a null model you constructed from my explanation?” Neither version of my article stated that numerical prediction. That does not prevent a critic deriving a consequence from an explanation I offered. It does require distinguishing my words from his reconstruction and supplying the assumptions the reconstruction needs.

Joel’s working draft does offer a rationale: if global stability merely preserved the initially random type order among equal-key records, final aggregation should remain at chance across duplicate multiplicities. A completed globally stable sort, with uniformly randomized type labels independent of the numerical-key arrangement, supports that endpoint benchmark. This is a legitimate conditional test of my complete stability account. “It never directly swaps equal keys” does not establish the global property that account needed. The peer-reviewed definition of stable sorting concerns preservation of equal-key relative order throughout the final output.

In Joel’s figure, “flat” refers to final aggregation plotted against copies per key, not to aggregation remaining constant at every moment during sorting. I do not attribute the latter prediction to that figure. An endpoint argument would not establish such a trajectory, but that limitation does not answer his actual comparison across duplicate multiplicities. My objections concern attributing the numerical forecast to my published words and carrying a successful objection to the old explanation over to the corrected argument.

For a linear array with exactly b labels of one type and s of another, let N = b + s ≥ 2, let H count matching neighboring pairs, and define aggregation as A = H/N, the measure used in Joel’s code. Uniform random arrangement gives:

E[A] = [b(b − 1) + s(s − 1)]/N².

There are N − 1 neighboring pairs. Each matches with probability [b(b − 1) + s(s − 1)]/[N(N − 1)]; dividing the expected matching-pair count by N gives the expression above. With equal fixed counts, this becomes E[A] = 1/2 − 1/N. For Joel’s 40/40, 80-cell experiment, it is 0.4875, rather than exactly 0.50. A denominator of N − 1, or independent coin-flip labels rather than fixed counts, gives a different finite-size answer.

The calculation was checked using exact rational arithmetic, independent C++ and Python enumeration, a recurrence over runs, symbolic simplification and seeded simulation. It supplies a conditional mathematical consequence. Joel does not need to establish that the actual mixed sort is globally stable before using it to test an explanation that says it is: departures from the benchmark and observed equal-key reversals can count against that explanation. The same stable-sort benchmark remains constant in expectation as duplicate multiplicity changes under these assumptions. Individual realized samples need not have identical scores.

The correction to 0.4875 does not rescue my old explanation from the large clustering effect. And it would be a mistake to dismiss Joel’s two-copy result merely because 0.4767 lies less than one reported standard error below that unconditional expectation. A more specific exploratory test fixes the observed type counts within every equal-value block and randomizes their internal order. Across the 15 two-copy runs, the observed total was 572 matching pairs, against a conditional expectation of 603.5. Exact counting gave a two-sided distance-from-expectation tail probability of approximately 0.00201; a separate 100,000-draw simulation gave 0.00208. That supports an ordering bias relative to this conditional reference. The comparison was chosen after inspecting the results, so confirmation requires predeclared or held-out tests.

Joel therefore has a valid objection to an appropriately specified complete stability account. Making its randomization assumptions and finite-size expectation explicit improves its precision. It remains a consequence he reconstructed, rather than a numerical forecast I published; the two experimental conditions are not two separately stated predictions of mine. The objection also leaves a different possibility open: movement policies can form clustering which tie handling subsequently helps retain. Rejecting my complete mechanism does not eliminate that narrower possibility or identify an independent Platonic cause.

A working draft repeats a related attribution problem

Joel Dietz sent me a working draft that uses reductionist framing for the blog critics and names me among them. That raises a related issue to my earlier exchange with Levin, although the two writers do not make identical accusations. The fuller exchange is discussed in my companion article on Levin’s response. The comparison concerns their representations of the argument; it establishes neither copying nor deliberate deception.

In my October 30, 2025 symposium comment, I explicitly defended higher-level causal explanation, citing the possibility that macro-level descriptions “can have higher effective information than micro-level descriptions.” Levin nevertheless answered a sentence about physical causal constitution with: “That’s a reductionist argument against the reality of other causal levels.” On the specific meaning he supplied, that attribution is demonstrably contradicted by the surrounding text: I was defending those levels. My physicalist wording could be challenged, and my proposed mechanisms could be wrong, without turning that defense into a denial. On November 11 he acknowledged his initial physicalist reading and asked me to clarify my alternative, a reasonable request. My comment; his October reply; his November clarification.

“Reductionism” needs a specified meaning here. The Stanford Encyclopedia’s account distinguishes questions about what exists from questions about explanation, and notes that reductionists commonly retain the reality of what they reduce. Its account of reductionism in biology also separates ontological, epistemic and methodological claims. I am not treating every reduction as elimination, or claiming that my preferred label settles these debates. The point is narrower: I did not argue that genuine higher-level patterns or explanations must disappear into a particle-level description.

The causal-emergence literature gives this distinction a concrete setting. Hoel, Albantakis and Tononi, and Hoel’s later treatment, compare causal models whose macro descriptions can outperform their micro descriptions under a specified information measure. Their results depend on how scales and interventions are defined; Eberhardt and Lee challenge aspects of that construction. Those papers do not establish my thermodynamic account or settle the ontology of causation. My quotations establish which position I defended; whether that position succeeds is a separate question.

Joel introduces “reductionist blog critics” and concludes by rejecting “reductionist dismissals that wave away real emergent phenomena without checking the math.” His criticism has a legitimate historical target in my overconfident February claim that familiar stability already explained the finding. His broader framing does not establish that my corrected argument denies clustering, novelty or mathematical explanation. The October 13 revision, preceding his October 25 invitation, explicitly withdrew the complete mechanism and acknowledged potentially novel results. Applying the dismissive characterization to that position requires identifying an argument I still make, rather than leaving the withdrawn explanation to represent it.

My surviving question concerns the further causal contribution. Joel quotes that question accurately elsewhere and assigns nonphysical ingress no incremental standing. His account of clustering through the implemented policies therefore contains a local point of agreement with my criticism. A disagreement about one mechanism does not erase that agreement, and agreement about those dynamics does not settle either writer’s wider metaphysics.

The “36,000 words” description is a different issue

The “36,000 words” description needs a different correction. In his December 28 Q&A, Levin describes a large body of comments, says he approved some, and explicitly presents his replies as answers to summarized points. Joel describes the material as containing “every possible argument one could make against his positions” and places it under discussion of AI-generated prose. That is Joel’s additional characterization. Neither the word count nor a general concern about AI establishes that a particular argument is mistaken, that all the material has the same authorship, or that the shorter list preserves every qualification.

I am not alleging that the word count is false or that either writer explicitly called it a single email. Length and suspected AI use can be legitimate moderation concerns. My own compression also produced real errors, which I acknowledge in the companion article. The issue relevant here is whether the argument being answered is the argument I actually made and still defend. A discarded stability explanation, an alleged rejection of higher-level causation, and my surviving question about independent Platonic causation are different targets. Answering one does not answer all three.

“Free compute” needs a specified bill

In the Lex clip, 13:06–13:40, Levin moves from clustering requiring no extra clustering instructions to the possibility of useful ingressions without extra physical costs. In the Curt Jaimungal clip, 2:16–2:35, he presents free compute as a testable prediction. There is a legitimate engineering question here: can one process provide several useful outputs for less cost than computing each separately?

If “free” means that the already-running process yields an additional useful pattern without a dedicated extra clustering routine, the observation can support that modest claim. Reusing work is useful. It needs no exemption from physics and supplies no identification of a nonphysical contributor.

A stronger efficiency claim needs a comparison. Fix the required outputs and their accuracy, the inputs and type assignment, and the implementation boundary. Then compare the mixed sort with serious ways of obtaining the same outputs, counting comparisons, movements, scheduling, memory, measurement and readout where relevant. If the claim concerns energy, measure or model the relevant physical costs. A procedure that explicitly recognizes types and commands clustering is one possible rival; its expense is not a lower bound on every mechanism that could produce the pattern.

Landauer’s principle adds no universal charge for every bit merely being present or for every comparison and swap. It constrains logically irreversible information loss under specified conditions. Conversely, failing to add a purpose-specific instruction does not demonstrate zero additional physical cost. The same trace can yield two observables without the second observable opening a hole in the energy budget. Landauer; Bennett.

Levin explicitly disclaims breaking physics in the Curt discussion. The issue is therefore the inference from a potentially useful shared computation to causal ingress, together with the unmeasured physical-cost claim. A measured advantage would deserve engineering credit. Its proposed Platonic source would still need separate support.

A rising score does not yet establish a detour

Joel’s distinction between two sorting measures is useful, provided neither is promoted into physical energy without justification. Let I(v) count all inverted pairs and E(v) count adjacent descents. In his example:

Swapping the adjacent inverted pair 4, 1 improves I while worsening E. Every strict adjacent inversion swap lowers I by exactly one; only that pair’s relative order changes. Both the example and this property were checked computationally, including exhaustive checks through eight distinct keys. A temporary decline in the paper’s adjacent-order score can therefore occur without frozen cells, anticipation, or a move uphill in I.

That does not make E an illegitimate observable. Nor does a mathematical Lyapunov function become the system’s thermodynamic energy merely by being called its “real energy.” The paper also compares behavior across defect conditions; an example without defects does not dispose of that comparative result. It does establish that a dip-and-recovery curve alone cannot distinguish obstacle-sensitive problem solving from the projection of ordinary sorting dynamics onto this score. An adequate comparison must test the defect response beyond that baseline and distinguish movable passive cells from genuinely immovable barriers.

The published study uses a minimal behavioral conception of intelligence, and explicitly separates its target from first-person affect or experience. The relevant question is whether its behavioral criteria establish the capacities claimed. Higher-level descriptions may earn predictive or interventionist value even when the dynamics are fully implemented. Demonstrating such value would support those descriptions; it would not, by itself, establish felt motivation or a separately acting nonphysical mind.

What would earn the additional causal claim?

There are at least three different proposals worth separating. Mathematical relations may constrain possible outcomes. A behavioral description may improve prediction or control. A nonphysical pattern may be proposed as an independently causally active source. Evidence for the first two cannot simply be booked to the third. A formal or constitutive interpretation of Platonic space deserves an argument appropriate to that interpretation; it need not posit a new force. If the claim instead identifies a further causal supplier, it must explain what warrants that identification.

For clustering, useful next tests would vary movement range, scheduling and tie behavior separately, with fresh inputs reserved for prediction. If “intrinsic motivation” means active maintenance of a clustering target, scramble type order within equal-value blocks while preserving numerical sortedness and test whether the system restores a specified pattern. Record what internal cell states the intervention preserves or changes. Restoration, passive retention and renewed formation are different results. Their interpretation must be fixed before the observations arrive.

For Platonic ingress, the required addition is a bridge from the proposed source to an independently specified pattern of outcomes or a defended explanatory advantage over serious rivals. Simply relabeling everything the dynamics produce as ingress offers no such comparison. If the programme instead earns its standing by inspiring productive experiments, count that success at the level of a research heuristic. Success at generating questions does not settle the ontology used to generate them. The same discipline applies to my preferred naturalistic accounts.

This is also where Joel’s working draft converges with the surviving criticism. His own technical account produces the clustering from the implemented policies, and his scoring gives nonphysical ingress no incremental standing. Those results can challenge my discarded mechanism while supporting my remaining demand for an evidential bridge. His composite headline percentage should not be read as a calibrated probability that an independent nonphysical cause exists.

Real clustering, novel behavior and useful higher-level explanations can all be granted without identifying an independent Platonic source. Evidence that defeats one inadequate mechanism narrows the search. It does not choose the winner by default.

What Levin Claims to Have Found

Watch carefully as the argument changes explanatory levels. He describes modified sorting systems producing behaviors beyond their explicit sorting task. He calls this phenomenon “clustering” and suggests it’s evidence of “free compute” and “intrinsic motivation” beyond the explicit sorting goal.

He does explain what “clustering” means: increased same-policy adjacency, not duplicate-key stability.

The god-of-the-gaps problem arises when “I didn’t expect this” becomes evidence for an independent nonphysical source. Levin acknowledges that the behavior follows the implemented rules. The unresolved step is why it should also earn causal credit for Platonic forms. It is trivial to claim everything is doing something extra when an unexpected consequence becomes a new ontological furniture.

What Bubble Sort Stability Explains

Computer scientists have a term for duplicate-key order preservation. It’s called algorithm stability, and it’s been documented in standard algorithms textbooks for decades.

A stable sorting algorithm preserves the relative order of elements with equal keys. When you sort [5, 3, 5, 1] and the two 5s end up adjacent to each other in their original relative order, that adjacency follows numerical sorting; their retained relative order is stability. That’s bubble sort doing exactly what bubble sort does, mechanically and deterministically, because ordinary stable bubble sort swaps adjacent elements only when they are strictly out of order.

Donald Knuth’s The Art of Computer Programming (1973) discusses stability. Thomas Cormen’s Introduction to Algorithms (1990) also discusses sorting and stability. Equal-key order preservation is undergraduate computer science. That familiarity does not establish that the mixed system’s particular algotype-clustering curves were already explained.

When equal elements retain their original relative order, that is stability. This is not evidence of an additional agency. This is not evidence of felt intrinsic motivation. This is what happens when a stable comparison-based sorting algorithm encounters equal keys; algotype clustering is a different question.

When you grant each element in a sorting array autonomous local decision-making capacity, you have changed the sorting architecture. You have built an agent-based model whose collective behavior still needs analysis. This is rather like announcing that you have found a new source of fire, neglecting to mention that you are standing in a room full of fireplaces, and declining to explain what the fireplaces leave for that source to explain.

Thomas Schelling, an economist, not a biologist, and therefore perhaps less susceptible to the temptations of Platonic mysticism, demonstrated in 1969, working with coins on a checkerboard, that agents following simple local preference rules can cluster into segregated groups as a consequence of local rules and starting conditions. He published the formal results in the American Economic Review in 1969 and the Journal of Mathematical Sociology in 1971. The result did not require conscious coins or a separate nonphysical supplier. He observed that local rules produce global clustering, wrote it up, and moved on.

The model demonstrated that large-scale segregation patterns emerge even when no individual agent desires segregation and even when the threshold for similarity preference is quite modest. The field that grew from this work, with its roots in von Neumann’s cellular automata from the early 1950s and the first formal simulation frameworks arriving by the mid-1960s, spent the subsequent half-century investigating this explanatory family: simple local rules can produce complex emergent clustering through their interactions and starting conditions. No additional agency is established by clustering alone. No independent Platonic cause is thereby established. No breathless announcement to the audience of a popular podcast required.

Reynolds showed with the Boids model that patterns such as group formation and clustering can be extremely sensitive to initial conditions while remaining entirely determined by simple, deterministic rules operating on local information alone. Conway showed it with the Game of Life. Wolfram devoted an entire book to it. The Santa Fe Institute has been publishing papers about it since before some of its current fellows were born. The observation that agents following different local strategies self-organize into clusters belongs to a familiar explanatory family. A particular clustering curve can still be a finding; familiarity with emergence does not derive it. Giving that result an independent nonphysical source is roughly equivalent to crediting water flowing downhill to an extra agency before showing what gravity leaves unexplained.

What concerns the honest observer is not surprise itself, which is how inquiry finds something worth explaining. What concerns the honest observer is surprise receiving the credit of a metaphysical discovery. The appropriate question to ask — “when you converted the sorting algorithm into an agentic system, did you not change the system being studied?” — is not difficult. Lex Fridman did raise the possibility of modeling and proving the clustering, and Levin agreed that the algorithm could be traced. The harder question survives: what does the observation establish beyond those implemented dynamics?

The mystery, to adapt a phrase, is not in the algorithm. The mystery is in the willingness to let wonder outrun the inquiry needed for causal attribution.

Implementation Questions Require Evidence

Claims about what the algorithm “does” need to distinguish the abstract rule, the distributed model, and its execution. The following are implementation questions, not demonstrated causes of the measured clustering.

Modern compilers perform aggressive optimizations. Where a compiler generates machine code from bubble sort, it may:

Unroll loops for performance

Reorder operations to exploit instruction-level parallelism

Use SIMD instructions to compare multiple elements simultaneously

Generate code executed on processors using branch prediction and speculative execution

Generate code whose execution interacts with cache hierarchies and timing

None of this is “mysterious.” None of this is “between the lines.” All of it is documented in compiler design textbooks and processor architecture manuals. Correct optimizations preserve defined program semantics; those mechanisms do not automatically explain logical algotype clustering.

If Levin observed behaviors that seemed to deviate from his expectations, one question worth asking is: “What optimizations is the compiler performing?” Another question is: “What are the hardware effects?” The central question is: “Did I understand what the algorithm actually does?”

Levin explicitly accepts that the sorting behavior can be traced through the algorithm. The question is why the same behavior should then establish a separate Platonic contribution. Naming hardware effects does not answer that question; neither does naming Platonic space.

The Company You Keep: How the Discovery Institute Found Their Newest Unwitting Ally

Let us examine, with the care it deserves, who has been paying enthusiastic attention to Levin’s Platonic morphospace argument. The Discovery Institute, the Seattle-based organization whose Center for Science and Culture promotes intelligent design, has published several articles celebrating Levin’s work as advancing their own research program. They have not been subtle about this. Their writers describe his Platonic space argument as evidence that “teleology advances,” and Brian Miller says Levin should “recognize the designer behind the design.” They are, in short, adding their designer to his proposed nonphysical source.

To his credit, Levin is not a proponent of intelligent design. The Discovery Institute’s coverage acknowledges that distinction even while celebrating the opening his proposal provides. You can disavow the company you keep. You cannot make an unsupported causal inference stronger by changing the proposed source. Their endorsement does not refute his view; the inferential bridge stands or falls on its evidence.

The older design inference

A documented early example is Illustra Media’s Unlocking the Mystery of Life, which the filmmaker dates to September 2003. Its official “Design Inference” excerpt features Paul Nelson, Stephen Meyer and William Dembski. In promoting the film in March 2004, the Discovery Institute described scientists abandoning naturalistic explanations for the origin of genetic information and turning to design. It treated information encoded in DNA as suggesting “the activity of a prior designing intelligence.” The film documents the public use of this argument in the early 2000s.

The strongest design argument is not exhausted by saying that something remains unexplained. It may also appeal to alleged positive evidence, such as an analogy with information produced by known intelligent agents. That argument requires its own assessment: whether the analogy holds, whether the rival explanations have been represented adequately, and whether the evidence favors the proposed designer. Merely calling it a gap would not answer those questions.

The parallel relevant here is narrower. An inadequate account does not select an additional supplier. Neither does the absence of an instruction explicitly prescribing a particular pattern. Sorting makes the second point unusually clear: an implementation can produce consequences that were never specified as goals. A program does not need a separate instruction announcing every property of its execution.

This also marks a difference between the cases. The documentary addresses the origin of biological information; the sorting experiment concerns an engineered system with published rules. Levin accepts that its behavior can be traced through those rules. His proposal is not the film’s designer hypothesis. The common burden arises wherever either proposal claims additional causal credit: what evidence or argument identifies the proposed source beyond the dynamics, constraints and alternatives already admitted?

Watch the official “Design Inference” clip.

Levin proposes that embodied forms and minds draw on a structured nonphysical space of patterns. That is a substantive hypothesis, not simply an assertion that every unexplained form must originate elsewhere. The vulnerable step arises when surprise, mathematical structure or the productivity of that hypothesis is credited as evidence identifying its independent causal source. Platonic space and an intelligent designer are different proposals; an explanatory gap selects neither. Each still owes an argument for its contribution beyond the strongest alternatives. Calling either proposal a research programme does not supply that argument.

Dembski’s specified complexity, Behe’s irreducible complexity and Levin’s Platonic space are different proposals. The Federation of Terminological Innovation may supply new stationery; it cannot supply the missing inference. An unexplained result does not select a nonphysical cause. Even granting one would not select Platonic patterns over an intelligent designer. Where both are merely compatible with the same result, that result discriminates neither. A new address for the gap is not a causal mechanism.

Discovery Institute writer Stephen J. Iacoboni describes Levin as endorsing ingress from an immaterial Platonic space with no naturalistic explanation. That is their interpretation, not a finding of the sorting study. They recognize an opening for their argument. The bubble sort demonstration supplies no such conclusion on its own. Used to identify an independent source merely because clustering was not an explicit goal, it repeats the vulnerable move: observe unexpected behavior, then credit an additional source before distinguishing its contribution from the implemented dynamics.

Levin’s empirical biology makes this distinction especially important. The bioelectric work is real. The xenobot findings are real. The planarian two-headed morphology research is real and has specific, testable, falsifiable implications. The overreach begins when that experimental success is credited to a Platonic causal source before its contribution has been distinguished, while thermodynamic constraint dynamics and organizational closure provide serious competing accounts of the relevant biological organization without requiring a non-physical realm whose distinctive causal contribution still needs discriminating support. If a gap is accepted as evidence of Platonic causation before alternatives have been distinguished, it can stifle inquiry: “why does this happen?” becomes “which form ingressed?” before the proposed source has earned its place. In biology, that risks mistaking an unfinished explanation for a finished answer.

Call this kind of move “the retreat into the marvelous.” When empirical investigation runs up against its current limits, the temptation is to fill the gap not with “I don’t know yet” but with a proposed source whose contribution has not been distinguished, and then to describe the proposal as a discovery. The bubble sort demonstration is the retreat into the marvelous at its most compressed: a familiar sorting rule, a measured agent-based clustering effect, and a leap from surprise to an independent source. The early documentary shows that intelligent-design advocates were already marketing this kind of inference decades ago. Repetition does not supply the additional designer’s missing evidence..

The Thermodynamic Reality Still Applies

Here’s what actually constrains and shapes algorithmic behavior, without establishing an independent Platonic supplier:

Energy dissipation: Physical sorting has implementation-dependent energetic costs. Landauer’s principle concerns logically irreversible erasure under specified conditions (kT ln 2 for an unbiased bit in the standard setting), not every comparison or swap. A swap can be logically reversible; counting comparisons and swaps alone does not determine dissipated joules.

Hardware constraints: The algorithm executes on physical substrates with real constraints: transistor switching times, wire delays, power limits, thermal budgets. These constraints shape execution at the implementation level; their contribution to a particular recorded pattern needs examination.

Implementation details: The programming language, compiler optimization level, processor architecture, memory hierarchy, and operating system scheduler all affect observable behavior. None of these are “mysterious.” All are documented.

Comparison function semantics: What counts as “equal” depends on how the comparison function is defined. Change the comparison function, and sorting behavior can change. Algotype clustering also depends on interactions among local policies and execution order. Executing those policies does not, by itself, identify an independent Platonic source.

Degrees of Freedom and the Source Claim

Levin makes much of his “degrees of freedom” argument, comparing algorithms to steganography, where you can hide information in bits that don’t affect the primary image. This analogy does not establish an independent source.

In steganography, you deliberately exploit redundancy to encode information. The “hidden” information exists because you put it there intentionally. In Levin’s bubble sort example, an additional observable pattern need not have been deliberately encoded. It can arise through interactions among the implemented rules. An extra useful output does not, by itself, require extra instructions or a nonphysical supplier.

When Levin allowed duplicate values to ease the sorting constraint, he created genuine degrees of freedom in the final arrangement of equal-valued cells. Those cells could remain grouped by algotype while the values were correctly sorted. Different policies can form segregation while strict tie handling can help retain it. That partial mechanism is distinct from global sorting stability. The endpoint tests support persistence in the tested model; they do not establish strict tie handling as its unique necessary mechanism or identify an independent nonphysical cause.

This is like noticing that when you remove constraints from a physical system, it explores more of its phase space, then concluding that the system must have “intrinsic motivation.” No. A system’s allowed trajectories depend on its transition rules and physical constraints. Relax a restriction, and more arrangements may become possible. That permits a phenomenon worth studying; it does not select a nonphysical explanation.

The YouTube comments quoted in the original article raise the methodological problem. Their authors’ credentials and explanations are not substitutes for the study:

What he’s actually discovered: Deterministic algorithms produce patterns that weren’t explicitly written as high-level goals. The error: He’s confusing ’emergent from the algorithm’s steps’ with ‘not explained by the algorithm.’ The clustering pattern IS the algorithm.”

The Platonic Bait-and-Switch

Here’s the inferential move that needs scrutiny in sorting and biological examples:

  1. Observe a phenomenon you did not expect
  2. Describe its unexpected competence
  3. Suggest an independent Platonic source
  4. Use the usefulness of the research framework to motivate the source
  5. Still owe evidence distinguishing causal ingression from an effective heuristic
  6. Repeat

The experiments are science. Without that discriminating step, the additional cause is metaphysics wearing the experiments’ lab coat.

The bubble sort “discovery” is particularly revealing because unlike biological systems (where complexity can hide mechanistic gaps), the modified sorting model has published rules and code. If the sorting result does not distinguish an independent Platonic source from implemented dynamics, why should we trust his metaphysical interpretations of vastly more complex biological phenomena?

What This Actually Demonstrates

The bubble sort episode demonstrates something genuinely important, but not what Levin thinks.

It demonstrates that even brilliant researchers can grant a metaphysical interpretation more credit than the experiment earns. Levin accepts mechanistic traceability, yet connects the unexpected behavior to Platonic space. The issue is not whether he knows computer science. It is whether that connection has been established.

This is the familiar temptation: the tendency to see mystery and transcendence wherever understanding runs thin, rather than acknowledging “I don’t know yet, let me investigate further.”

What the Evidence Establishes

Here is what the mechanisms and evidence establish, without pretending to have derived every clustering curve:

Bubble sort stability: Ordinary adjacent bubble sort with strict inversion swaps preserves equal-key relative order. Merely refusing direct equal-key swaps does not guarantee global stability in a mixed system with nonadjacent swaps. Formation of algotype segregation and retention within tied blocks remain separate questions.

Compiler effects: High-level code is transformed by the compiler into machine code that may exhibit behaviors not obvious from reading the source. Correct optimizations preserve defined semantics; their role in this recorded clustering would require evidence.

Hardware effects: Modern processors reorder instructions, predict branches, and exploit parallelism in ways that create observable patterns. All of this is documented in processor manuals.

Thermodynamic constraints: Physical execution requires physical resources. Landauer’s bound applies to the relevant irreversible information operations, not every operation indiscriminately. Those constraints do not supply a derivation of algotype clustering.

Higher-level explanation: Describing collective organization, mathematical constraints or causal patterns at a useful scale can be legitimate explanatory work. Demonstrating a mechanism need not make those descriptions dispensable. Their success also does not, on its own, identify a further independently acting Platonic source.

No independent source established: The study and Levin’s interview acknowledge that the behavior follows the rules. An additional output can arise without an additional explicit goal. My positive mechanism claim required its own evidence; my request for the proposed source’s evidential warrant does not depend on that mechanism succeeding. Failure of my explanation does not identify his added source. Nor does an account that omits that source prove its universal absence.

Mysticism Is Not Discovery

Michael Levin’s sorting demonstration establishes unexpected collective behavior. It does not establish that an independent nonphysical pattern supplied it. The policies, starting conditions, and execution are available for investigation; the extra cause still needs its own evidential receipt.

This is not how science works. This is how gap-filling works.

Christopher Hitchens might have asked Levin one simple question: “What observation would count against your proposed cause?” Levin’s later paper does offer conditions for abandoning its research programme. The sharper question is whether a test distinguishes independent Platonic causation from a useful model or an intelligent-design reinterpretation of the same observations. A productive framework can earn its keep without its preferred ontology inheriting the result.

The sorting results expose limits in what we have characterized about these machines. They justify further investigation. When that finding is offered as evidence for an independent nonphysical cause, the appropriate response is to ask what establishes the proposed source beyond the implemented dynamics and serious alternatives.

The mystery isn’t in the algorithm. The mystery is why the extra cause should get the discovery’s credit.

Equal-key stability and algotype clustering are different phenomena.

Compiler, runtime, and hardware effects matter where they affect the recorded dynamics.

An extra useful output does not establish an extra nonphysical cause.

The experiment must distinguish the proposed source before its success is credited to that source.
