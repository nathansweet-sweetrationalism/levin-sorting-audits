This article predates several later clarifications
and extensions of my methodology and working ontology.
I am leaving the original argument intact for
intellectual provenance rather than silently
rewriting the record. The later work makes my
claim typing, scope limits, bridge requirements,
uncertainty bookkeeping, and comparative standards

My work on
 
Thermodynamic Monism
 
was developed as a naturalistic process-relational
research programme and comparative countermodel,
particularly in response to Platonic and
anti-physicalist arguments claiming that biological
organization, morphogenesis, cognition, or other
phenomena required additional nonphysical explanatory
machinery. Its purpose was to test how much of the
relevant explanatory work could be recovered from
causal continuity, thermodynamic constraint,
historically accumulated organization, multiscale
dynamics, and higher-level difference-making without
assuming the richer metaphysical posit under dispute. 
I did not take that comparative success to establish
that thermodynamics explains everything, that
thermodynamic variables are privileged at every
scale, or that empirical success by itself settles
the intrinsic nature of reality. The current
formulation makes those limits more explicit.
The commitments I continue to retain include
one causally continuous process order rather than
separate causal substances or realms; thermodynamic
constraints on physical realization; dynamically
maintained and historically conditioned organization;
and the reality of higher-level explanatory variables
when they earn predictive, counterfactual,
interventionist, compressive, or transferable leverage.
 One process does not mean one explanation. 
The same clarification applies to my earlier use
of  falsification-first  language.
I did not use the rule “if it is not falsifiable,
it is false.” My methodology already distinguished
questions of semantic determinacy, evidential burden,
theory-ladenness, auxiliary hypotheses,
counterfactual testing, and Lakatosian
research-programme appraisal. A sufficiently
determinate empirical or causal claim should incur
conditions under which it would lose support,
but not every meaningful claim belongs before
the same tribunal. Formal, semantic,
phenomenological, historical, modal, normative,
ontological, causal, and empirical claims can
require different forms of warrant and criticism. 
My current
 
Recursive Corrigibility Framework
 
formalizes that plural structure more explicitly.
The shift from  falsification-first 
to  corrigibility-first  is therefore
primarily a clarification and expansion of jurisdiction:
identify the claim type, determine what evidence or
argument actually bears on it, locate the relevant
bridge and dependencies, specify what could revise it,
and localize failure to the part of the argument that
actually failed rather than allowing one failure to
automatically propagate through an entire framework. 
My current working ontology is
 
Provisional, Boundary- and Scale-Relative Realism
 .
It makes explicit a discipline already motivating
much of the earlier work: boundaries, scales,
identities, causal variables, and explanatory
vocabularies must earn their relevance at the
target under investigation. Local success does
not automatically transport into universal,
intrinsic, necessary, or exhaustive
metaphysical conclusions. 
An accessible statement of that position appears in
 
 What Is Provisional, Boundary- and
Scale-Relative Realism? 
 .
Explanatory gaps are treated as records of missing
warrant rather than automatic evidence for whatever
ontology might fill them. Evidence also does not
transport across boundaries, scales, or constructs
merely because the same vocabulary can be used
in both places. 
My earlier consciousness work should be read with
the same distinction in mind. My proposed bridge
between organized physical dynamics and phenomenality
was already presented as a risky empirical wager
with explicit conditions for failure, not as a
settled conclusion about phenomenal identity.
Phenomenal identity remained unresolved. What later
work makes more explicit is the dependency structure.
Evidence for organizational closure, recursive
integration, bounded perspective, temporal continuity,
report, discrimination, self-modeling, valuation,
or agency can bear strongly on those particular
capacities without automatically settling whether
any of them is identical with phenomenal experience.
Evidence earned by one construct should not
silently migrate into another. 
I therefore now decompose the traditional Hard
Problem more explicitly into distinct questions
about phenomenal occurrence, phenomenal character,
candidate subject boundaries, physical organization,
report and judgment, the meta-problem, modal arguments,
bridge principles, and explanatory completion.
This preserves unresolved phenomenality without
allowing the unresolved remainder itself to perform
explanatory or metaphysical work that has not
independently been earned. The Hard Problem dissolves
as a distinct unitary problem if, once those
constituent questions are separated, no additional
positive explanandum remains and no necessary
explanatory relation is lost by the decomposition. 
The most current statement of that analysis is
 
 David Chalmers’s “Hard Problem of Consciousness”
Has a Hard Problem of the Gaps: How His Own
Methodology Dissolves Both 
 .
The newer argument asks whether the canonical
Hard Problem retains any additional positive
explanandum after its constituent questions are
separated. If no positive residual can be identified,
and no necessary explanatory relation is destroyed
by the decomposition, then the monolithic Hard
Problem dissolves as a distinct unitary explanandum
while the constituent questions remain live
wherever the evidence leaves them unresolved. 
My later comparison with Matthew David Segall
and Whiteheadian panexperientialism applies the
same rule in another direction. In
 
 Where the Necessity Case for Mathew Segall
& Whitehead’s Prehension Fails 
 ,
I ask which explanatory targets actually require
subjective prehension rather than merely remaining
compatible with it. Mechanistic, dispositional,
process, organizational, constraint-based, and
intervention-sensitive accounts recover substantial
causal explanatory work without subjective prehension
as a premise. That undercuts a necessity claim at
those targets without establishing that universal
prehension is false. Its wider application still
requires independent comparative and transport warrant. 
My October work on Michael Levin extends a distinction
that was already central to my earlier criticism of
biological Platonism: strong empirical results do not
automatically transfer their evidential authority
to every metaphysical interpretation placed around
them. In
 
 Religion With A Pipette: Is Michael Levin’s

A Scholarly Analysis

I separate the experimental evidence for
bioelectricity, morphogenesis, biological competency,
synthetic organisms, and multiscale control from
the additional claim that such systems access
nonphysical minds or Platonic structures. 
The newer formulation makes the credit-assignment
rule especially explicit: evidence shared by
multiple rival explanations supports the shared
empirical structure first. An additional metaphysical
posit earns additional credit only when the feature
it uniquely adds improves prediction, intervention,
explanation, compression, transfer, or some other
claim-appropriate discriminator relative to
serious alternatives.

Feynman Christopher Hitchens would have eviscerated this bubble sort argument in three sentences, but I’ll need more space because the inferential overreach here is layered like a particularly overripe onion. Michael Levin, whose legitimate work on bioelectricity deserves respect, and his collaborators report unexpected collective behaviors in distributed sorting systems built from familiar policies. Those findings deserve analysis. He connects them to Platonic space; the connection is what needs evidence. Treating surprise as proof of an independent source is a category error wrapped in breathless mysticism and sold as profundity, and the “beyond the algorithm” argument risks the same inferential move as intelligent design when it credits an additional source without distinguishing it from the implemented system (local rules, inputs, scheduling, hardware, etc.). The title names a pattern of reasoning. Levin rejects intelligent design and agrees that the sorting behavior follows the implemented rules. My criticism applies when an unanticipated result, the absence of an explicit clustering goal, or the failure of one proposed mechanism is credited as support for an independent Platonic source. Those facts do not identify that source. Mathematical constraint, a useful behavioral model and an independently causally active pattern are different claims. The task is not the complete algorithm There are several different objects being called “the algorithm”: the instruction to sort numerical values; the actual local policies, state variables, scheduling and stopping rules; and the trajectory produced by those rules from a particular starting state. The sorting goal does not specify every feature of that trajectory. A complete implementation can determine features that its designer never listed as goals. In the  Lex Fridman clip , Levin accepts that someone could track the clustering through the algorithm. He then describes the additional behavior as “neither prescribed nor forbidden by the algorithm” (14:46–15:28 in the clip;  02:18:32 onward in the full interview ). That wording needs a stable referent. Clustering may be unspecified by the sorting task while remaining a consequence of the complete implemented dynamics. Moving between those senses makes a consequence of the rules sound as though it occupies a causal vacancy between them. 
 Write the implemented update schematically as  x(t + 1) = F(x(t), u(t)) , where  x  includes cell values, types and internal state, and  u  includes the scheduled choices and any random input. Once these are fixed, the model fixes the trajectory. Aggregation is another function of that state,  A(t) = g(x(t)) . There need be no instruction saying “maximize g” for g to rise. This does not make every consequence obvious, easy to prove, or computationally cheap to predict. The published study makes a related inference when it says clustering is “not something that can be predicted a priori,” because cells do not read algotype information (Section 4.4). But type already determines the policy a cell executes. Policy affects its movements; movements affect who becomes adjacent. The causal route can run through behavior without a direct query for a neighbor’s type. Absence of such a query does not establish an impossibility of prediction. Clustering formation and clustering persistence are different questions My original stability explanation conflated them. A globally stable sort preserves the starting relative order of every equal-key record. Strict comparisons alone do not guarantee that property in a mixed system containing nonadjacent swaps. In  [2a, 2b, 1c] , swapping the first and last records produces  [1c, 2b, 2a] . The equal records have reversed without being directly swapped with each other. In the replay of Joel’s model, every one of the 90 duplicate-key runs reversed some equal-key records. The mixed model therefore lacks the global stability my complete explanation would require. Clustering with unique values also needs an explanation independent of equal-key preservation. Those objections to my old account stand. But duplicate keys still loosen the final ordering constraint. Different policies can create segregation during sorting, and strict tie handling can help retain it when numerical sorting is complete. Continuing the actual final cells with their existing internal states and unchanged strict rules produced no further swaps in all 105 replayed runs. This establishes persistence at those completed endpoints in the tested implementation. At ten copies per key, the mixed model’s mean final aggregation was  0.6592 . Applying a globally stable numerical sort to the same starting records instead gave  0.503333 . Uniformly permuting algotypes only within the final equal-value blocks, while preserving each block’s type counts, gave an exact conditional mean of  0.485117 . Numerical sortedness survives this last operation while much of the measured segregation does not. The arrangement within tied blocks matters. These comparisons distinguish formation from retention. They do not establish strict tie handling as the unique necessary mechanism, and the within-block permutation is not Joel’s separate experiment allowing equal-key swaps. Nor is replaying his sequential model an independent replication of the original threaded implementation. What survives my first explanation is this narrower role in persistence. My objections concern attributing the numerical forecast to my published words and carrying a successful objection to the old explanation over to the corrected argument. For a linear array with exactly  b  labels of one type and  s  of another, let  N = b + s ≥ 2 , let  H  count matching neighboring pairs, and define aggregation as  A = H/N , the measure used in Joel’s code. Uniform random arrangement gives: E[A] = [b(b − 1) + s(s − 1)]/N². There are  N − 1  neighboring pairs. Each matches with probability  [b(b − 1) + s(s − 1)]/[N(N − 1)] ; dividing the expected matching-pair count by  N  gives the expression above. With equal fixed counts, this becomes  E[A] = 1/2 − 1/N . For Joel’s 40/40, 80-cell experiment, it is  0.4875 , rather than exactly 0.50. A denominator of  N − 1 , or independent coin-flip labels rather than fixed counts, gives a different finite-size answer. The calculation was checked using exact rational arithmetic, independent C++ and Python enumeration, a recurrence over runs, symbolic simplification and seeded simulation. It supplies a conditional mathematical consequence. Joel does not need to establish that the actual mixed sort is globally stable before using it to test an explanation that says it is: departures from the benchmark and observed equal-key reversals can count against that explanation. The same stable-sort benchmark remains constant in expectation as duplicate multiplicity changes under these assumptions. Individual realized samples need not have identical scores. The correction to 0.4875 does not rescue my old explanation from the large clustering effect. And it would be a mistake to dismiss Joel’s two-copy result merely because 0.4767 lies less than one reported standard error below that unconditional expectation. A more specific exploratory test fixes the observed type counts within every equal-value block and randomizes their internal order. Across the 15 two-copy runs, the observed total was  572  matching pairs, against a conditional expectation of  603.5 . Exact counting gave a two-sided distance-from-expectation tail probability of approximately  0.00201 ; a separate 100,000-draw simulation gave  0.00208 . That supports an ordering bias relative to this conditional reference. The comparison was chosen after inspecting the results, so confirmation requires predeclared or held-out tests. Joel therefore has a valid objection to an appropriately specified complete stability account. Making its randomization assumptions and finite-size expectation explicit improves its precision. It remains a consequence he reconstructed, rather than a numerical forecast I published; the two experimental conditions are not two separately stated predictions of mine. What Bubble Sort Stability Explains Computer scientists have a term for duplicate-key order preservation. It’s called algorithm stability, and it’s been documented in standard algorithms textbooks for decades. A stable sorting algorithm preserves the relative order of elements with equal keys. When you sort [5, 3, 5, 1] and the two 5s end up adjacent to each other in their original relative order, that adjacency follows numerical sorting; their retained relative order is stability. That’s bubble sort doing exactly what bubble sort does, mechanically and deterministically, because ordinary stable bubble sort swaps adjacent elements only when they are strictly out of order. Donald Knuth’s The Art of Computer Programming (1973) discusses stability. Thomas Cormen’s Introduction to Algorithms (1990) also discusses sorting and stability. Equal-key order preservation is undergraduate computer science. That familiarity does not establish that the mixed system’s particular algotype-clustering curves were already explained. When equal elements retain their original relative order, that is stability. This is not evidence of an additional agency. This is not evidence of felt intrinsic motivation. This is what happens when a stable comparison-based sorting algorithm encounters equal keys; algotype clustering is a different question. When you grant each element in a sorting array autonomous local decision-making capacity, you have changed the sorting architecture. You have built an agent-based model whose collective behavior still needs analysis. This is rather like announcing that you have found a new source of fire, neglecting to mention that you are standing in a room full of fireplaces, and declining to explain what the fireplaces leave for that source to explain. Thomas Schelling, an economist, not a biologist, and therefore perhaps less susceptible to the temptations of Platonic mysticism, demonstrated in 1969, working with coins on a checkerboard, that agents following simple local preference rules can cluster into segregated groups as a consequence of local rules and starting conditions. He published the formal results in the American Economic Review in 1969 and the Journal of Mathematical Sociology in 1971. The result did not require conscious coins or a separate nonphysical supplier. He observed that local rules produce global clustering, wrote it up, and moved on. The model demonstrated that large-scale segregation patterns emerge even when no individual agent desires segregation and even when the threshold for similarity preference is quite modest. The field that grew from this work, with its roots in von Neumann’s cellular automata from the early 1950s and the first formal simulation frameworks arriving by the mid-1960s, spent the subsequent half-century investigating this explanatory family: simple local rules can produce complex emergent clustering through their interactions and starting conditions. No additional agency is established by clustering alone. No independent Platonic cause is thereby established. No breathless announcement to the audience of a popular podcast required. Reynolds  showed with the Boids model that patterns such as group formation and clustering can be extremely sensitive to initial conditions while remaining entirely determined by simple, deterministic rules operating on local information alone. Conway showed it with the Game of Life. Wolfram devoted an entire book to it. The Santa Fe Institute has been publishing papers about it since before some of its current fellows were born. The observation that agents following different local strategies self-organize into clusters belongs to a familiar explanatory family. A particular clustering curve can still be a finding; familiarity with emergence does not derive it. Giving that result an independent nonphysical source is roughly equivalent to crediting water flowing downhill to an extra agency before showing what gravity leaves unexplained. What concerns the honest observer is not surprise itself, which is how inquiry finds something worth explaining. What concerns the honest observer is surprise receiving the credit of a metaphysical discovery. The appropriate question to ask — “when you converted the sorting algorithm into an agentic system, did you not change the system being studied?” — is not difficult.  Lex Fridman  did raise the possibility of modeling and proving the clustering, and Levin agreed that the algorithm could be traced. The harder question survives: what does the observation establish beyond those implemented dynamics? The mystery, to adapt a phrase, is not in the algorithm. The mystery is in the willingness to let wonder outrun the inquiry needed for causal attribution. Implementation Questions Require Evidence Claims about what the algorithm “does” need to distinguish the abstract rule, the distributed model, and its execution. The following are implementation questions, not demonstrated causes of the measured clustering. Modern compilers perform aggressive optimizations. Where a compiler generates machine code from bubble sort, it may: Unroll loops for performance Reorder operations to exploit instruction-level parallelism Use SIMD instructions to compare multiple elements simultaneously Generate code executed on processors using branch prediction and speculative execution Generate code whose execution interacts with cache hierarchies and timing None of this is “mysterious.” None of this is “between the lines.” All of it is documented in compiler design textbooks and processor architecture manuals. Correct optimizations preserve defined program semantics; those mechanisms do not automatically explain logical algotype clustering. If Levin observed behaviors that seemed to deviate from his expectations, one question worth asking is: “What optimizations is the compiler performing?” Another question is: “What are the hardware effects?” The central question is: “Did I understand what the algorithm actually does?” Levin explicitly accepts that the sorting behavior can be traced through the algorithm. The question is why the same behavior should then establish a separate Platonic contribution. Naming hardware effects does not answer that question; neither does naming Platonic space. The Company You Keep: How the Discovery Institute Found Their Newest Unwitting Ally Let us examine, with the care it deserves, who has been paying enthusiastic attention to Levin’s Platonic morphospace argument. The Discovery Institute, the Seattle-based organization whose  Center for Science and Culture  promotes intelligent design, has published several articles celebrating Levin’s work as advancing their own research program. They have not been subtle about this. Their writers describe his Platonic space argument as evidence that “ teleology advances ,” and  Brian Miller  says Levin should “recognize the designer behind the design.” They are, in short, adding their designer to his proposed nonphysical source. To his credit, Levin is not a proponent of intelligent design. The Discovery Institute’s coverage acknowledges that distinction even while celebrating the opening his proposal provides. You can disavow the company you keep. You cannot make an unsupported causal inference stronger by changing the proposed source. Their endorsement does not refute his view; the inferential bridge stands or falls on its evidence. The older design inference A documented early example is Illustra Media’s  Unlocking the Mystery of Life , which the filmmaker dates to  September 2003 . Its official  “Design Inference” excerpt  features Paul Nelson, Stephen Meyer and William Dembski. In  promoting the film in March 2004 , the Discovery Institute described scientists abandoning naturalistic explanations for the origin of genetic information and turning to design. It treated information encoded in DNA as suggesting “the activity of a prior designing intelligence.” The film documents the public use of this argument in the early 2000s. The strongest design argument is not exhausted by saying that something remains unexplained. It may also appeal to alleged positive evidence, such as an analogy with information produced by known intelligent agents. That argument requires its own assessment: whether the analogy holds, whether the rival explanations have been represented adequately, and whether the evidence favors the proposed designer. Merely calling it a gap would not answer those questions. The parallel relevant here is narrower. An inadequate account does not select an additional supplier. Neither does the absence of an instruction explicitly prescribing a particular pattern. Sorting makes the second point unusually clear: an implementation can produce consequences that were never specified as goals. A program does not need a separate instruction announcing every property of its execution. This also marks a difference between the cases. The documentary addresses the origin of biological information; the sorting experiment concerns an engineered system with published rules. Levin accepts that its behavior can be traced through those rules. His proposal is not the film’s designer hypothesis. The common burden arises wherever either proposal claims additional causal credit: what evidence or argument identifies the proposed source beyond the dynamics, constraints and alternatives already admitted? Watch the official “Design Inference” clip . 
 Levin proposes that embodied forms and minds draw on a structured nonphysical space of patterns. That is a substantive hypothesis, not simply an assertion that every unexplained form must originate elsewhere. The vulnerable step arises when surprise, mathematical structure or the productivity of that hypothesis is credited as evidence identifying its independent causal source. Platonic space and an intelligent designer are different proposals; an explanatory gap selects neither. Each still owes an argument for its contribution beyond the strongest alternatives. Calling either proposal a research programme does not supply that argument. Dembski’s specified complexity, Behe’s irreducible complexity and Levin’s Platonic space are different proposals. The Federation of Terminological Innovation may supply new stationery; it cannot supply the missing inference. An unexplained result does not select a nonphysical cause. Even granting one would not select Platonic patterns over an intelligent designer. Where both are merely compatible with the same result, that result discriminates neither. A new address for the gap is not a causal mechanism. Discovery Institute writer  Stephen J. Iacoboni  describes Levin as endorsing ingress from an immaterial Platonic space with no naturalistic explanation. That is their interpretation, not a finding of the sorting study. They recognize an opening for their argument. The bubble sort demonstration supplies no such conclusion on its own. Used to identify an independent source merely because clustering was not an explicit goal, it repeats the vulnerable move: observe unexpected behavior, then credit an additional source before distinguishing its contribution from the implemented dynamics. Levin’s empirical biology makes this distinction especially important. The bioelectric work is real. The xenobot findings are real. The planarian two-headed morphology research is real and has specific, testable, falsifiable implications. The overreach begins when that experimental success is credited to a Platonic causal source before its contribution has been distinguished, while thermodynamic constraint dynamics and organizational closure provide serious competing accounts of the relevant biological organization without requiring a non-physical realm whose distinctive causal contribution still needs discriminating support. If a gap is accepted as evidence of Platonic causation before alternatives have been distinguished, it can stifle inquiry: “why does this happen?” becomes “which form ingressed?” before the proposed source has earned its place. In biology, that risks mistaking an unfinished explanation for a finished answer. Call this kind of move “the retreat into the marvelous.” When empirical investigation runs up against its current limits, the temptation is to fill the gap not with “I don’t know yet” but with a proposed source whose contribution has not been distinguished, and then to describe the proposal as a discovery. The bubble sort demonstration is the retreat into the marvelous at its most compressed: a familiar sorting rule, a measured agent-based clustering effect, and a leap from surprise to an independent source. The early documentary shows that intelligent-design advocates were already marketing this kind of inference decades ago. Repetition does not supply the additional designer’s missing evidence.. The Thermodynamic Reality Still Applies Here’s what actually constrains and shapes algorithmic behavior, without establishing an independent Platonic supplier: Energy dissipation: Physical sorting has implementation-dependent energetic costs. Landauer’s principle concerns logically irreversible erasure under specified conditions (kT ln 2 for an unbiased bit in the standard setting), not every comparison or swap. A swap can be logically reversible; counting comparisons and swaps alone does not determine dissipated joules. Hardware constraints: The algorithm executes on physical substrates with real constraints: transistor switching times, wire delays, power limits, thermal budgets. These constraints shape execution at the implementation level; their contribution to a particular recorded pattern needs examination. Implementation details: The programming language, compiler optimization level, processor architecture, memory hierarchy, and operating system scheduler all affect observable behavior. None of these are “mysterious.” All are documented. Comparison function semantics: What counts as “equal” depends on how the comparison function is defined. Change the comparison function, and sorting behavior can change. Algotype clustering also depends on interactions among local policies and execution order. Executing those policies does not, by itself, identify an independent Platonic source. Degrees of Freedom and the Source Claim Levin makes much of his “degrees of freedom” argument, comparing algorithms to steganography, where you can hide information in bits that don’t affect the primary image. This analogy does not establish an independent source. In steganography, you deliberately exploit redundancy to encode information. The “hidden” information exists because you put it there intentionally. In Levin’s bubble sort example, an additional observable pattern need not have been deliberately encoded. It can arise through interactions among the implemented rules. An extra useful output does not, by itself, require extra instructions or a nonphysical supplier. When Levin allowed duplicate values to ease the sorting constraint, he created genuine degrees of freedom in the final arrangement of equal-valued cells. Those cells could remain grouped by algotype while the values were correctly sorted. Different policies can form segregation while strict tie handling can help retain it. That partial mechanism is distinct from global sorting stability. The endpoint tests support persistence in the tested model; they do not establish strict tie handling as its unique necessary mechanism or identify an independent nonphysical cause. This is like noticing that when you remove constraints from a physical system, it explores more of its phase space, then concluding that the system must have “intrinsic motivation.” No. A system’s allowed trajectories depend on its transition rules and physical constraints. Relax a restriction, and more arrangements may become possible. That permits a phenomenon worth studying; it does not select a nonphysical explanation. The Commentariat Sees Through It The YouTube comments quoted in the original article raise the methodological problem. Their authors’ credentials and explanations are not substitutes for the study: MattBuske writes: “Actual Computer Scientist here, I have some concerns and I think Levin has made a fundamental conceptual error that invalidates his entire argument. What he’s actually discovered: Deterministic algorithms produce patterns that weren’t explicitly written as high-level goals. That seems a massive stretch.” The Platonic Bait-and-Switch Here’s the inferential move that needs scrutiny in sorting and biological examples: Observe a phenomenon you did not expect Describe its unexpected competence Suggest an independent Platonic source Use the usefulness of the research framework to motivate the source Still owe evidence distinguishing causal ingression from an effective heuristic Repeat The experiments are science. Without that discriminating step, the additional cause is metaphysics wearing the experiments’ lab coat. The bubble sort “discovery” is particularly revealing because unlike biological systems (where complexity can hide mechanistic gaps), the modified sorting model has published rules and code. If the sorting result does not distinguish an independent Platonic source from implemented dynamics, why should we trust his metaphysical interpretations of vastly more complex biological phenomena? What This Actually Demonstrates The bubble sort episode demonstrates something genuinely important, but not what Levin thinks. It demonstrates that even brilliant researchers can grant a metaphysical interpretation more credit than the experiment earns. Levin accepts mechanistic traceability, yet connects the unexpected behavior to Platonic space. The issue is not whether he knows computer science. It is whether that connection has been established. This is the familiar temptation: the tendency to see mystery and transcendence wherever understanding runs thin, rather than acknowledging “I don’t know yet, let me investigate further.” What the Evidence Establishes Here is what the mechanisms and evidence establish, without pretending to have derived every clustering curve: Bubble sort stability: Ordinary adjacent bubble sort with strict inversion swaps preserves equal-key relative order. Merely refusing direct equal-key swaps does not guarantee global stability in a mixed system with nonadjacent swaps. Formation of algotype segregation and retention within tied blocks remain separate questions. Compiler effects: High-level code is transformed by the compiler into machine code that may exhibit behaviors not obvious from reading the source. Correct optimizations preserve defined semantics; their role in this recorded clustering would require evidence. Hardware effects: Modern processors reorder instructions, predict branches, and exploit parallelism in ways that create observable patterns. All of this is documented in processor manuals. Thermodynamic constraints: Physical execution requires physical resources. Landauer’s bound applies to the relevant irreversible information operations, not every operation indiscriminately. Those constraints do not supply a derivation of algotype clustering. Higher-level explanation: Describing collective organization, mathematical constraints or causal patterns at a useful scale can be legitimate explanatory work. Demonstrating a mechanism need not make those descriptions dispensable. Their success also does not, on its own, identify a further independently acting Platonic source. No independent source established: The study and Levin’s interview acknowledge that the behavior follows the rules. An additional output can arise without an additional explicit goal. My positive mechanism claim required its own evidence; my request for the proposed source’s evidential warrant does not depend on that mechanism succeeding. Failure of my explanation does not identify his added source. Nor does an account that omits that source prove its universal absence. Mysticism Is Not Discovery Michael Levin’s sorting demonstration establishes unexpected collective behavior. It does not establish that an independent nonphysical pattern supplied it. The policies, starting conditions, and execution are available for investigation; the extra cause still needs its own evidential receipt. This is not how science works. This is how gap-filling works. Christopher Hitchens might have asked Levin one simple question: “What observation would count against your proposed cause?” Levin’s later paper does offer conditions for abandoning its research programme. The sharper question is whether a test distinguishes independent Platonic causation from a useful model or an intelligent-design reinterpretation of the same observations. A productive framework can earn its keep without its preferred ontology inheriting the result. The sorting results expose limits in what we have characterized about these machines. They justify further investigation. When that finding is offered as evidence for an independent nonphysical cause, the appropriate response is to ask what establishes the proposed source beyond the implemented dynamics and serious alternatives. The mystery isn’t in the algorithm. The mystery is why the extra cause should get the discovery’s credit.
